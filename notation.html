Lesson 3

Reading and writing chess

Chess has its own alphabet. Once you can read it, every chess book, every puzzle and every game in history is open to you, and you can write down your own games to look at later.

Every square has a name

Files (up and down) are letters a to h, starting from White's left. Ranks (across) are numbers 1 to 8, starting from White's side. The letter always comes first: e4, never 4e.

Every piece has a letter

PieceLetterExample
KingKKe2: king moves to e2
QueenQQh5: queen moves to h5
RookRRd1: rook moves to d1
BishopBBc4: bishop moves to c4
KnightNNf3: knight moves to f3
Pawn(nothing)e4: a pawn moves to e4

Why is the knight an N? Because K was already taken by the king. The knight got the second letter of its name instead.

When two identical pieces could both go to the same square, add where the piece came from: Nbd2 means "the knight on the b-file goes to d2", and R1e2 means "the rook on the first rank goes to e2".

A whole game on one line

Here is a real (short!) game written in notation, next to the board it produces. Press Next and follow along on the scoresheet.

#WhiteBlack
1.e4e5
2.Bc4Nc6
3.Qh5Nf6??
4.Qxf7#1–0

The 1–0 at the end means White won. 0–1 means Black won, and ½–½ means a draw.

Behaving well at the board

Chess players have looked after each other's games for hundreds of years. These are the rules of the club, and they are also the rules of every tournament you will ever play in.

  • Shake hands before and after every game. Say "good game" whether you won or lost.
  • Touch-move. If you touch one of your pieces, you must move it if there is a legal move. If you let go of it on a square, the move is finished.
  • Say "I adjust" before you straighten a piece on its square, so nobody thinks you are moving it. You should not adjust your pieces or mess with the board while it is your opponent's time to play.
  • Stay quiet. No talking about the game, no advice from watchers, no sound effects.
  • Do not gloat and do not sulk. Everyone loses games. What people remember is how you handled it.
  • Resigning is allowed when the game is truly lost, and it is polite. But at your level, play on: opponents drop pieces and stalemate happens.
  • If something goes wrong (an illegal move, a knocked-over piece, a disagreement), stop, do not argue, and raise your hand for an adult.

Clocks. We do not use them at club, so take the time you need to think. You will meet one in a tournament: press it with the same hand you moved with, right after your move. If your flag falls and your opponent still has enough pieces to checkmate, you lose on time.

Write down your own games

Feel free to bring a notebook to club. Writing your moves down takes a few seconds each turn and gives you the ability to look at your own game afterwards and find the exact move where things went wrong. Every strong player in the world does this.
